Oxidation-state curves predict formation energies beyond binary oxides

FEFOS predicts a compound's formation energy from the binary compounds of its elements alone. This report checks that the rewritten code reproduces the 2023 paper, then tests the extension to any number of cations against every multi-cation compound in Materials Project with one of twelve anions.

The idea holds and the extension works. On ionic compounds with metal cations, across all twelve anions, FEFOS predicts formation energies with a mean absolute error of eV/atom. That is half the error of the reference average without charge transfer (). The error does not grow from two cations to five or more.

How FEFOS predicts an energy

  1. One curve per element and anion. Every binary compound MaXb sits at a formal oxidation state q = v·b/a, where v is the anion valence. The lower convex hull of formation energy against q describes how the element responds to oxidation and reduction.
  2. A common reference. All cations of a compound start at its mean oxidation state. The reference energy is the composition-weighted average of each element's hull at that state (Eq. 6 with ΔD = 0).
  3. Charge-neutral redistribution. Cations shift their oxidation states subject to Σ ci di = 0 (Eq. 2), and each shift is priced by that element's curve. The minimum is the mixing energy ΔD. Version 2 solves this exactly for any number of cations.

Three variants are compared throughout. In the linear mode the curve is the hull itself. The quadratic mode uses the paper's Eq. 1 parabolas, a1x² + a2x, fitted to the hull and weighted by concentration. The paper mode keeps the 2023 merging rule, an unweighted sum with the end-member correction of Eq. 5, generalised to n cations. In every mode the curves are fitted automatically, so the eight hand-set coefficients of 2023 are gone.

The 2023 paper, reproduced

The legacy module reruns the original binary mixing rule with the published coefficients and reference oxides. It reproduces all published predictions to within eV/atom, which is the rounding of the published table. The errors against the 2022 MP energies match the paper: eV/atom overall and for the coordination-filtered oxides. Exact agreement needed two details of the original notebook: concentrations were rounded to two decimals and snapped to a 0.02 grid, and the end-member subtraction was done separately for each oxidation direction.

Any anion, any number of cations

The extension was scored on every multi-cation compound in MP whose most electronegative element is one of twelve anions. The fitting window for the quadratic and paper modes was chosen on the 2023 domain only and then applied unchanged everywhere else, so every result outside two-cation oxides is out of sample. The headline scope is ionic compounds with metal cations: mean oxidation state of at least 1, with no nonmetal acting as a cation.

What the benchmark says about the method

Charge neutrality is the right way to merge curves

Redistributing oxidation states under charge neutrality brings the error below the reference average for every anion, every cation count and every class of compound, and halves it for ionic compounds overall. This is the core FEFOS idea, and it carries over from binary oxides without change.

The exact merge is the convex hull of the binaries

With the hull itself as the curve, the charge-neutral minimum equals the energy of the most stable mixture of binary compounds. It agrees with pymatgen's phase-diagram code to 10−14 eV/atom on 394 random compounds, and its Lagrange multiplier is the anion chemical potential. The paper's parabolas are a smooth two-coefficient summary of that hull, at a cost of about 0.016 eV/atom.

Weight the curves by concentration

The 2023 rule sums the curves without concentration weights. That works for binary oxides but roughly doubles the error on bromides and iodides relative to the linear mode. Concentration weights are what thermodynamics requires, and they are the default in v2.

What is still missing

Real compounds are more stable than their binary decomposition products: the linear prediction sits eV/atom above MP on average for stable ionic compounds. Electronegativity spread explains little of this gap (r = ). Size and charge contrast from assumed bond lengths explain more, as the next section shows. Polyanions remain the main open problem.

Assumed bond lengths

The paper pointed to ionic size, through tolerance factors and the τ descriptor, as the next ingredient. FEFOS can supply sizes without the structure of the compound being predicted. Each of near-hull binary compounds gives a reference cation–anion bond length at the cation's charge. Neighbours come from CrystalNN, as in Materials Project, and charges from bond valence, so Li counts as +1 in Li2O2. Interpolating the references in charge gives an assumed bond length d(M, X, q) for any charge, across element–anion pairs. Evaluated at the oxidation states FEFOS predicts, it gives every cation a size from composition alone.

Two descriptors follow from the assumed lengths: the size mismatch of the cations (the spread of d relative to its mean) and the spread of their ionic potential q/d, an acid–base scale. Both correlate with the energy FEFOS misses, with r = and for compounds near the hull, against −0.14 for electronegativity. Compounds with more contrast are more stable than their binary decomposition products. A linear correction with three coefficients per anion (an offset and one slope per descriptor) was fitted on near-hull compounds and scored only on compounds it never saw.
